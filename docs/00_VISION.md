# Moonshot Inc — Vision & Pillars

*The "why." What experience we're building and what makes it worth playing. Read this first.*

---

## The One-Liner

A Steam-first business simulation where you found a frontier-tech company, raise venture capital, build it from a garage to an IPO, and grow into a magnate who shapes whole industries. Coffee Inc 2's depth meets Crusader Kings' sense of a living world — with a Bloomberg-terminal UI that's actually a pleasure to use.

## The Fantasy

The emotional arc is the whole point: **Founder → Executive → Owner → Capital Allocator.**

You start as a founder with an idea and a little money. You build a product, hire a team, fight for every dollar. You raise a seed round and learn what it costs in ownership. You scale — and stop doing the work yourself, learning to delegate to executives. You take the company public, or sell it. And then, wealthy and proven, you do it again — or you start investing in *other* founders, allocating capital across an ecosystem you used to compete in. The game is the story of becoming someone who moves markets.

Most business sims start you rich and ask you to get richer. That's the mistake Wall Street Raider made (see below). Moonshot starts you small and makes the *building* the point — the net-worth milestones, the first unicorn, the first IPO bell, the first time you write an angel check. Those earned moments are the spine.

## Who It's For

- **Sim/strategy players** who want real systemic depth (the Coffee Inc 2, Capitalism Lab, Crusader Kings audience)
- **Finance-curious players** who find cap tables, term sheets, and market cycles genuinely fun — and players who want to *learn* those things by playing
- **Steam desktop players** first; a mobile companion comes later as a separate product
- Two-tier accessibility: a newcomer reads the colors and categories and plays a good game; a finance nerd drills into every number and plays a deep one. Neither is locked out of the other's experience.

## The Four Pillars

1. **A living world, not a spreadsheet.** Master-variable-driven macro cycles, sector hype, an interconnected economy of companies with real relationships, a news layer that's load-bearing, and (stretch goal) a visual 2D-isometric office/campus that grows with your company. The numbers are consequences of a world that's happening, not the world itself.

2. **UI-first, always.** The single hardest-won lesson of the genre: depth is necessary but not sufficient. A brilliant simulation with a bad interface is a cult game, not a hit. Every screen is designed and A/B-tested before it's built. The cap table and the fundraising negotiation are hero features that must be *perfect*.

3. **Strategic depth over punishment.** You can always start another company. Losing teaches ("productive losing" — every loss should be reconstructable in hindsight, never arbitrary). Soft signals over shown probabilities everywhere — pattern recognition beats optimization. Dramatic mechanics are gated to rare earned moments so they never become routine.

4. **Maximum narrative, all optional.** Text-only (no voice acting, ever). A CEO log, team activity, relational news, and character moments make the world feel inhabited — and every word of it is skippable for the player who just wants the data.

## The North-Star Cautionary Tale: Wall Street Raider

WSR had — by reputation — the deepest financial simulation ever built, proven educational value (players got finance careers from it), and devoted fans. It stayed an obscure cult game **for 40 years** for one reason: the interface. Multiple well-funded teams (including a studio that did Disney work, spending a year and hundreds of thousands of dollars on an iPad port) tried to modernize it and *all failed*.

The lessons we've internalized from it:
- **Depth is necessary but not sufficient** — UI-first is the correct strategy, never let it slip when build pressure hits.
- **"Read the 300-page manual" is a failure state** — depth must be discoverable through play (the new-game flow, contextual hints, in-context decisions all exist for this).
- **The motivational arc WSR lacked** — starting rich has no "why"; our founder→magnate arc is the spine WSR never had.
- **The news ticker is load-bearing** — reward attentive players with information that matters.
- **The interconnected economy** — companies with real relationships make the world feel alive (our light company relationship system).

## What "Done" Looks Like for V1

A Steam game where a player can: pick an industry and sub-industry, found a company, raise multiple rounds through a real negotiation, run the company through a living economy with a signature mechanic that makes their sub-industry feel distinct, delegate to executives at their chosen pace, hit ~50 events that tie the systems together, and exit via IPO or acquisition — then do it again with persistent founder reputation. Single company at a time, two industries (AI + Space) with three sub-industries each, English-only, with the architecture in place for everything else to layer on.

## The Tone

Newsroom-meets-Bloomberg. Headlines like "Q3 Earnings Beat Sends [Company] Stock Soaring," not gamey notifications. The player is treated as an adult role-playing an executive. Warm where it counts (the human/personal layer), serious where it counts (the financial simulation), never condescending.
